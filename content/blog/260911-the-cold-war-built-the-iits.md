The usual story of the IITs is about vision. A new country decided to build world-class engineering schools, and decades later those schools powered India's software boom.

That story isn't wrong. It just leaves out the part that explains why it worked.

A friend walked me through the other version, and I went and checked the details afterwards. The short version is that nobody in it was acting out of generosity.

## Everyone wanted something

In the 1950s, India had just become independent and hadn't picked a side in the Cold War. That made it worth courting.

IIT Kharagpur, the first one, opened in 1951 as an Indian effort. The next few came with foreign help, and the list reads like a map of Cold War influence:

- IIT Bombay was set up with support from the Soviet Union, through UNESCO.
- IIT Madras was built with help from West Germany.
- IIT Kanpur got help from a consortium of American universities.
- IIT Delhi was supported by the United Kingdom.

Each of these countries had its own reasons. The US didn't want India drifting toward Soviet communism. The Soviets wanted influence in Asia. Aid for education was a cheap and visible way to buy goodwill.

The smart move on India's side was accepting all of it. Letting rival powers compete to fund you got the country several institutions it probably couldn't have paid for alone.

## Built to resist the local pressure

The part I found more interesting is how the IITs were protected.

In a system where influence usually decides who gets what, a well-funded institution in the middle of a city is a target. Powerful local families would want their children admitted. Politicians would want a say in hiring.

The IITs were set up as institutes of national importance, with large residential campuses that ran almost like self-contained towns, and admission through a common entrance exam. That structure didn't make them perfect. But it gave them a better chance of staying merit-based than most public institutions of the time.

## Good graduates, no jobs

Then came a problem nobody had solved. India was producing excellent engineers and had a closed, slow-growing economy with very few jobs for them.

So many left. A large share of IIT graduates in those decades went to the US, where the tech industry was growing fast and needed people. In India this got called brain drain, and it was treated as a loss.

It turned out to be an early investment.

## The satellite dish

In 1985, Texas Instruments set up a development centre in Bangalore and, after a long wait for government permissions, got a direct satellite link to send work back to the US. That was the proof that software could be built in India and delivered abroad without anything physical moving.

In 1991, the government created Software Technology Parks of India. They gave software exporters satellite data links, simpler rules and tax breaks. If you had engineers and a connection, you could sell services to a company on the other side of the world.

Now the pieces fit. American companies needed more engineering work. India had trained engineers and low costs. Indians who had gone to the US years earlier were now managers there, and they knew what the graduates back home could do.

## Money changes policy

Once foreign money started flowing in, the government couldn't treat the software industry as a side project. It was bringing in foreign exchange and taxes, and paying salaries that created a new middle class.

So the rules loosened a little more, then more after that, because the industry was paying the bills.

## A chain, not a plan

Laid out end to end:

Rival powers funded schools to win influence. The schools produced engineers with nowhere to work. The engineers left for the US. Demand grew there. Satellite links made remote work possible. Policy followed the money.

No single person designed that outcome. At every step, someone acted in their own interest, and the result happened to be one of the biggest shifts in India's economy.

I find that more useful than the vision story. The vision story tells you to wait for good leaders. The chain story tells you to look at who wants what, and where their interests line up with yours.

Most of what looks like progress, looked at closely enough, is someone's self-interest pointing in a useful direction. The question worth asking about any system is what it's accidentally making possible.
