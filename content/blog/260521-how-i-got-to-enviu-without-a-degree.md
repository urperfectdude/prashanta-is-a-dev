I started earning money right after 10th grade, during the COVID lockdown, at a computer shop in town.

I wasn't fixing anything complicated. Reinstalling Windows, cleaning dust out of old desktops, troubleshooting whatever a customer walked in complaining about. Most days it was the same three or four problems wearing different faces. But I was doing it myself, getting paid for it, and paying my own fees with that money. That mattered more than the work itself, because it meant if something broke, I already knew I could be the one who fixes it.

I didn't have a plan after that. I just kept saying yes to whatever came next. A UI design internship with a company overseas. Freelance video editing for people I'd met online. No-code app and web projects for anyone who'd pay for one. None of it connected on paper. Each one taught me something the last one hadn't.

Somewhere in there I noticed a problem with myself that I didn't have a name for yet. I was interested in too many things.

Not distraction, not the kind of thing where you bounce between hobbies out of boredom. Real interest, in things that had nothing to do with each other. Biology, nutrition, how the body actually works. Strength training, sports, the discipline both demand. Music production. Space, for no reason I can fully explain. Part of me still wants a pilot's license one day, just to know what flying feels like from the inside.

For a long time this felt like a flaw. Every time I got serious about one thing, I'd wonder if I should have gone all in on another. What if I'd taken biology seriously and gone toward medicine. What if I'd stayed in media instead of drifting into code. Each path felt genuinely worth doing, which made picking any single one feel like losing the others for good.

What changed wasn't that I finally picked one. It's that I noticed I'd been asking the wrong question. I wasn't looking for the one right field. I was looking for something that would let me keep being more than one version of myself at different times, without having to shut a door for good. Tech turned out to be that, not because it's the trendy answer, but because the same base skills let me move from app work to media work to whatever I'm doing now, without starting over each time.

While most people my age were studying for exams and going through placement season, I was spending my time in online communities instead. Not as an expert, mostly just around, answering questions I half knew the answer to, sharing whatever I'd figured out that week, watching people who were actually building things. I wasn't impressive in those spaces. But being around people who were shipping real products, working remotely, and making up their own path as they went changed how I thought a career was supposed to work. It stopped looking like a ladder with fixed rungs and started looking like something you build out of whatever you'd already learned, one piece at a time.

I'm 21 now. I work at Enviu. It's been eight months. I'm often the youngest person in the room, and I still don't have a degree.

If you'd told the version of me reinstalling Windows for the fourth time that week where this would lead, I don't think he'd have believed you. There wasn't a map for it. There still isn't, really. I just kept stacking whatever I could learn on top of whatever I'd already learned, and followed the interests I couldn't talk myself out of instead of the one that looked most sensible on paper.
