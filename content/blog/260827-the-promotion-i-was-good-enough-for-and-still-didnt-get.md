Good work does not carry itself to the people who decide what happens to it. I used to think it did.

For years the belief was simple: if you're good at the job, that's the whole formula. Ship clean work, solve the technical problem in front of you, and the rest sorts itself out. Talking to people, building relationships outside your immediate scope, being visible to anyone who wasn't already on your team, all of that felt like a distraction from the real work.

At a previous company, that belief cost me. There was a real promotion on the table, one I had an actual shot at getting on merit. It didn't happen. Not because the code was weak or the output was thin. Because I kept my head down, focused on the work in front of me, and never put in the time to talk to people, build relationships inside the company, or make sure anyone outside my immediate team knew what I was doing.

Nobody blocked me. Nobody undervalued the work on purpose. The decision simply got made by people I'd barely spoken to, based on a picture of me that was thinner than the work itself.

That's the part I misjudged. Technical output has a ceiling on its own. It proves you're competent. It does not make you the obvious choice when a room full of people who don't see your day to day has to decide who moves up. That decision runs on trust and visibility, not a changelog.

Collaboration isn't a soft skill sitting next to the real skill. It's the delivery mechanism for the real skill. Skip it, and your best work stays exactly where you built it: known to you, and maybe your closest teammates, invisible to everyone who actually signs off on what happens next.

I don't think of talking to people as networking anymore. I think of it as finishing the job. The build isn't done when it ships. It's done when the people who matter actually know it happened.

So the question I ask myself now isn't whether the work is good enough. It's who else knows.
