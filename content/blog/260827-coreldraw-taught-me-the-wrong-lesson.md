I was eleven or twelve when I decided CorelDRAW was going to make me good at something.

I'd sit for hours in that interface, learning where everything was, how to get a clean vector path without redoing it twice. Then Illustrator. Then Photoshop. I told myself once I knew the software well enough, I'd be a real graphic designer. The software was the skill. Learn the tool, own the outcome.

I was wrong about the mechanism, not the ambition.

A decade later I can open Photoshop and still not produce anything close to good design. Knowing the tool never made me a designer. It made me someone who knew where the buttons were.

What I skipped was the part that actually builds taste. Looking at layouts and asking why one works and another doesn't. Understanding hierarchy and spacing before you ever touch a mouse. That's not software knowledge. That transfers.

Tools don't transfer. They expire.

I found this out early, not from theory, from watching it happen to something I'd sunk real hours into. Photoshop is not the default tool for making graphics anymore. AI is. All the menu locations and shortcuts I memorized as a kid are irrelevant to a prompt box. If tool mastery had actually been the skill, mine would be worth nothing right now.

The fundamentals aged fine. Composition still means something. So does knowing when a design is trying too hard. Those didn't get replaced by AI. They just got a faster pair of hands.

So the real question was never which tool to learn. It was what I understood well enough that the tool became irrelevant. I built the wrong half for years before I noticed.

If you're deciding what to spend the next year on, ask which one you're actually buying. A tool rents you speed for a while. Fundamentals you keep.
