When I was volunteering in Auroville, I met a German woman who mentioned, almost casually, that she usually leaves her job and goes travelling for a while. She has done this multiple times and has been to almost 10 countries, each time looking to experience something new. This time she had come to India to learn Ayurveda in Kerala.

She said it the way someone here would say they are taking a week off for a cousin's wedding. Nothing dramatic about it.

My first reaction was not "wow, that's nice." My first reaction was, doesn't she worry?

Because that is how I have been trained to think about money and work. If you leave your job, the savings start going down from the first month. There is a gap on the resume that some interviewer will ask about later. What if the next job takes six months to find? What if it takes a year? And what if you fall sick in between, when there is no company insurance anymore and the hospital bill comes straight out of your own account?

For most Indian middle class families, quitting a job without another one lined up is a risk you take only when something has really gone wrong. And quitting just to travel would sound slightly irresponsible to most parents I know.

So I kept thinking, how is she so relaxed about this?

Then I started reading about how it actually works in Germany, and the math looked very different from what I had in my head.

In Germany, if you have been paying into unemployment insurance for long enough, you get unemployment benefit when you lose your job. It's around 60% of your previous net salary, and for most people under 50 it runs for up to a year. While you are getting it, the employment agency pays your health insurance contributions. So the two things I was most worried about on her behalf, income and hospital bills, are mostly covered by the system.

It isn't a free holiday though. If you quit voluntarily, you usually get a blocking period of up to 12 weeks where no benefit is paid, and the total benefit period also gets shorter. So she still needs some savings. But needing three months of savings is a very different problem from needing to survive with no floor at all for as long as the job search takes.

You could see that in how they travelled too. She and other travellers like her made their own breakfast: oats with hot water, vegan protein powder and some fruits. The fruits at least made it tastier. It looked like people making their money last.

Not everything was about money though. She told me she would be going from Bangalore to Pune in a non-AC sleeper train. I was honestly shocked. To me that sounded crazy.

She also prefers non-AC rooms and non-AC places in general. Her reason was that the body adapts to the temperature, so stepping outside doesn't feel so extreme.

The second part was about jobs.

My first thought was that European countries have less population, so there are more jobs to go around. That's not exactly right. Germany alone has more than 80 million people, which is not a small country. The real difference is how many people are competing for each job. Germany's unemployment rate is low compared to most rich countries. Its workforce is also getting older, so fields like nursing and the skilled trades struggle to find enough people. If you leave a job there, there is a decent chance another one will be waiting when you come back.

Compare that to India, where railway recruitment exams regularly get crores of applicants for a few tens of thousands of posts. When that is the level of competition, a gap on your resume feels like a much bigger deal, because there is always someone else ready to take the seat you left.

And then there is the currency. A few months in India on euro savings goes a lot further than the same money spent back home.

I don't want to make it sound like every European lives like this. I met one person. It also varies a lot by country. Youth unemployment in Spain, Italy and Greece is several times higher than in Germany, so a young person there probably can't be this relaxed about leaving a job. And Europe has its own costs. Rent in the big cities is painful. The safety net isn't free either, it comes out of people's salaries every month as taxes and contributions.

We also had a really good conversation about India and other countries, and about China becoming a superpower. In her view, people in China think about contributing to their country's growth in a way she hadn't seen elsewhere.

Her example was a sky ladder, basically a ladder fixed to the side of a cliff that you climb up. She said there was one in Austria, made with European equipment, but it wasn't open to everyone because it wasn't considered safe enough for just anyone to use. In China, she said, a similar one was open to everyone. No training, no specialisation, anyone could go up.

She also told me that an ex-boyfriend used to tease her that she could never climb that kind of ladder. Now she climbs them.

But I think I understood something about why her attitude felt so strange to me.

The risk she was taking is simply a different risk from the one I was imagining. When the system catches you if you fall, taking a break is a reasonable decision. When nothing catches you, the same decision looks reckless, and you end up building your whole life around never falling.

A lot of what we call being practical in India is really just being careful because we have to be.

I still don't know what I would do with that kind of safety net. I would like to think I would travel more. But I have a feeling I would still keep checking my bank balance every week anyway.
