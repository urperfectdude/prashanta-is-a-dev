A clean resume used to tell you something about the candidate.

Now it tells you they have the same tools as everyone else.

## The old filters are gone

A few years ago, you could screen applications on surface signals. English that wasn't quite right. A layout that looked homemade. A cover letter nobody had proofread.

Those filters were unfair. But they were filters.

Today everyone writes perfect English. Everyone runs their resume past an ATS checker. Everyone has a generated headshot and a tidy portfolio. A role that got 50 applications now gets 500, and most of them look the same.

A friend put the math plainly when we were talking about my own applications: the need to stand out is much bigger than it was a few years ago, because the pile is ten times bigger and it all reads alike.

## Tool fluency is the floor

The instinct is to list the tools. Built this with Claude Code. Designed that with AI.

My friend's reaction to that was blunt. Tell a company you used Claude Code and their first thought isn't "impressive." It's "so did the other 499."

Using the tools well is expected now. Listing them is like listing that you can send email.

The same goes for side projects. Building an app used to prove effort. When an app takes one night, the app proves very little. And you're competing with college students who have more free time than you and are just as hungry for the job.

## Where the signal went

When producing something gets cheap, the value moves to whatever can't be produced on demand. I wrote about the same shift in software itself in [Software Is Becoming Marketing](/blog/260715-software-is-becoming-marketing). Resumes are going through it too.

A few things are still hard to generate:

**Evidence that something was used.** A live link with real users says more than a repo or a screenshot. Nobody can check whether a feature description is true. They can check whether the thing works.

**Specific decisions.** A model can write "improved onboarding." It can't know which step you cut, why you cut it, or what you gave up to do it. The more specific the trade-off, the harder it is to fake.

**Work other people can see.** The same friend told me what set them apart early in their career wasn't the projects on their resume. It was everything around them: talks, articles, open source contributions. Work that existed in public, where someone else could check it.

**Combinations.** Being specialized used to be enough. Now every specialty has a deep pool of people. What's rare is someone who sits across two or three of them and can explain why that matters for this role.

That last one cuts both ways for me. A resume with design, engineering and consulting on it can read as scattered. It can also be the rare combination. The difference is whether the page makes the connection obvious, or leaves the reader to guess.

## What this means in practice

Polish is no longer a way to stand out. It's a way to avoid getting filtered.

The effort goes into proof instead. Fewer projects with real usage. Fewer bullet points with more specific decisions in them. Something public that a stranger can find without your resume pointing them to it.

If a model could have written your resume, making it sound better isn't the fix. The better question is what you've done that a model couldn't have written about.
