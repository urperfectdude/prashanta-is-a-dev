We tend to think wages go up when a society gets richer. They don't. They go up when the people paying them have to compete.

A friend explained this to me over a long call, using an example I wouldn't have picked: the Black Death.

## A plague that raised wages

Between 1347 and 1351, the plague killed somewhere between a third and half of Europe. Before it, a peasant had no bargaining power. Landlords had more workers than they needed, and a worker who asked for more could be replaced by the next person in line.

After it, fields sat full of crops with nobody to harvest them.

That changed the math for every landlord. If his crop rotted, he lost the whole season and still owed rent and debts. So when a worker said the landlord next door was offering double, he couldn't just refuse. Paying double was cheaper than losing everything. And once one landlord paid more, the next one had to outbid him.

England tried to stop this with a law. The Statute of Labourers in 1351 capped wages at pre-plague levels. It mostly failed. You can't legislate away a shortage when every employer privately needs to break the rule.

Workers ended up with more money and fewer mouths to feed. Some of that surplus turned into free time, and people with free time start thinking, building and questioning things. Historians argue about how directly this led to the Renaissance, but few argue that the labour shortage didn't reshape the economy that came after it.

## A village where nothing moves

Now take a village in India, fifty years ago, maybe less.

One family owns most of the land. Almost everyone else works on it. The landlord knows that when winter comes, people will need work to eat, and they have nowhere else to go.

The landlord in the next village knows the same thing. So they don't need a written agreement. The understanding is simple: I won't pay more than a hundred rupees a day, and neither will you. If someone breaks ranks and offers a hundred and fifty, the others make sure it costs him.

That's a stable system. Nobody in power has any reason to change it.

In India, caste made it worse, because it made the arrangement hereditary. A family worked someone's field, and so would their children, and their children's children. It went beyond money too. You didn't look the landlord in the eye. You stepped off the road when he passed. If you didn't, your family could lose its only source of work, and there was no other landlord who would hire you.

The Black Death broke Europe's equilibrium by removing workers. India had no such shock. So the equilibrium held.

## The shock that did come

What finally cracked it was trains, cities and eventually a phone in every pocket.

A young man from that village can now move to a city, earn there and send money home. The landlord hasn't changed. His power has. The dependency that held the whole system together is gone, and there's nobody he can complain to about it.

My friend described the landlords watching a labourer's son buy a new tool and not bother to bow. They are angry about it, and they can't do anything.

## Why people keep choosing cities

It's easy to say cities are bad for poor migrants. The wages are low, the housing is terrible, the work is hard.

All true. But people are not stupid about their own lives. If villages offered a better deal, the flow would run the other way. It doesn't. People move toward less friction, and for a lot of families, a hard city job with no master still has less friction than a village where one family decides whether you eat.

The city is just the first place where someone else might bid for your work.

## The same rule, at national scale

India ran a version of this for the whole economy. From independence until 1991, starting a business needed layers of government licences, imports were heavily restricted, and foreign companies were mostly kept out. That era is called the License Raj.

Local companies had protected markets, so they had little reason to get better. The system sat in equilibrium, the same way the village did.

The shock came in 1991. India's foreign reserves dropped to roughly three weeks of imports. The government pledged its gold, flew tonnes of it to London as collateral, and borrowed. The crisis forced the reforms that opened the economy. Competition came in, and so did the jobs and salaries that a lot of people my age grew up assuming were normal.

They opened the economy because they had run out of options.

## What I take from this

Stable systems rarely reform themselves. The people who benefit from them are the people who would have to change them.

Change usually shows up as a shock, and it only helps ordinary people when it gives them somewhere else to go.

So when I look at any job market, including my own, I ask one small question: who else would hire me tomorrow, and do they know that?
