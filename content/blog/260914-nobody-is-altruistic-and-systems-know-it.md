I used to believe that good systems come from good people. Put decent leaders in charge and things improve.

A friend recommended a book that took that belief apart: *The Dictator's Handbook* by Bruce Bueno de Mesquita and Alastair Smith.

## The main idea

The book starts from one assumption. Every leader, in every system, wants to get power and keep it. Not some leaders. All of them.

What changes between systems is how many people a leader needs to keep happy to stay in power. The authors call this group the winning coalition.

A dictator might need the support of a few generals, some senior officials and the people running the money. That's a small group. The cheapest way to keep them loyal is to pay them directly: contracts, positions, a share of whatever the country earns. Everyone else can be ignored, or kept down.

A democratic leader needs millions of votes. You can't bribe millions of people individually. So the cheapest way to stay in power is to provide things that help large numbers of people at once: roads, schools, clean water, a working economy.

## The part that bothered me

Here is the conclusion I didn't want to accept.

The democratic leader is not a better person than the dictator. Put the dictator in a democracy and they'd likely build roads too. Put the democrat in charge of a country run by five generals and they'd likely pay the generals.

Behaviour comes from the structure much more than from character. Who holds a stake in the outcome, and how many of them there are, decides what a rational leader does.

Character still matters, but it's a weak thing to build a system on, because you can't count on it being there.

## You can't build on goodwill

The same logic applies well outside politics.

If you start a company and your plan depends on people working hard because they believe in the mission, it won't last. A few people will. Most won't, and that isn't a flaw in them. They have rent, families and their own lives. A system that needs selflessness to function has a single point of failure.

The companies that work build the incentives in. Pay, ownership, recognition, career paths. They make the thing you want people to do also the thing that's good for them to do.

## The event gift

I noticed this in a small way a while ago. A company I was following was running a community event, and it offered a participation gift to everyone who joined.

My first reaction was confusion. Why pay people to show up to something good? Of course people would support it.

But that reaction said more about me than about the event. The company understood something I didn't: most people won't show up for free, however good the cause is. The gift was realistic design.

## Don't design for yourself

This was the hardest lesson for me personally.

The friend who recommended the book spends a lot of time thinking about how to be useful to people beyond their own circle. They were honest about it. Part of why anyone can think that way is that their own basics are covered. When you're just trying to get through the month, concern for strangers is a luxury.

Then they said the line I keep coming back to: "I'm an exception. I shouldn't model the world around people like me."

I recognised myself in that. People who care about the bigger picture tend to assume everyone else does too, if only someone explained it well enough. That's the mistake outliers make. They model the world on themselves. If I design a product, a team or a community on the assumption that people will act the way I would, it will fail, and I'll be confused about why.

## What I'd take from the book

Stop asking whether the people in charge are good. Ask who they depend on, and what those people want.

When a leader, a manager or a company does something that looks selfish or strange, it's usually a reasonable response to the people they answer to.

The useful question is how to build something where ordinary, self-interested people end up doing the right thing anyway.
