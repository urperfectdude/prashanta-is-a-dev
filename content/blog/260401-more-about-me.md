I dropped out of college with no backup plan. What followed was less a plan and more me trying on different work until something felt true.

Design was first. It didn't take. I still had media and video skills from years of editing for fun, so I built a portfolio and posted it on Twitter. That got me real offers, from MotorOctane, Finance with Sharan, and ThinkSchool, channels I used to watch, not pitch to.

Video editing didn't stick either, even though one of the biggest YouTube creators in India offered me a full-time job as a video editor around then. I had the chops, I could cut a genuinely good video, but I'd already gone cold on the process by the time the offer landed, so I turned it down. I tried running a design and media agency for a while too, mostly because it felt like the natural next step. Turns out I'm bad at managing people, or at least I was then. So I went the other direction entirely and took an Electrical Engineering role at L&T Construction, the highest package in my college batch, at nineteen.

I left within a month. Corporate life at nineteen felt like it would kill my curiosity and whatever ambitious thing I actually wanted to do, and I couldn't picture myself doing it long term anyway. The whole detour left me with one line I still repeat: coding doesn't make money, solutions do. My parents had opinions about all of this, delivered the way parents deliver opinions, as jokes that weren't really jokes.

Next I went at hardware. Courses, a portable body cooler, a payment tracker, none of which went anywhere. Then Foodaspas, a social app for foodies, which I worked on for about six months before admitting to myself it was never going to ship for real users. Yeh sab gile sikwe leke mai nikla safar pe.

What followed was travel: mountains, cities, villages, more of India than I'd seen before, still a fraction of what I'd planned but enough to change how I think. Somewhere in there I also picked up the value of no-code for moving fast, worked alongside people like Aditya Pati who were pushing India's no-code community forward, and ended up consulting on low-code EV operations for Driveo, which went well for a few months.

Then came Jagruti Yatra, fifteen days on a train built to show you India's diversity through the people actually living it. No more consuming business case studies from a screen. Actually sitting with entrepreneurs and hearing it straight from them. Grateful for how all of it added up, even the parts that didn't work.

tldr; small-town kid, addicted to the internet, dropped out of IIT Madras. The real syllabus was whatever rabbit hole was open: Bubble docs at 2am, Flutter screens, EV dashboards, LLM SQL hacks, ML papers, Premiere timelines, the occasional standup set.

Chose tech because I have too many real interests to fit into one lane: biology, physiology, evolution, nutrition, medicine, sports, media, strength training, flying, and space. Instead of picking a single field, I picked a base flexible enough to hold all of them, so I get the freedom to keep evolving instead of locking into one fixed identity.

## Somethings I believe in

- Ask forgiveness, not permission. Clarity shows up after you start, never before. Check in before every step and you turn into a headache for whoever's paying you to act, not someone actually moving.

- There's no right time, only the next one. Your second attempt will beat your first. Read "The Subtle Art of Not Giving a F*ck" if you want the long version: you suck at the start, and sucking is the whole mechanism.

- Motion isn't direction, and speed only helps when you're pointed right. Watch the feedback, not the movement. Execute, check if you're actually headed anywhere, then adjust. Skip that middle step and you're just moving fast in the wrong direction.

- Iteration times consistency is what compounds. Neither one alone does much.

- Whatever you can control is a skill issue. Whatever you can't, ignore it. Most people spend their energy complaining about the part they can't touch instead of fixing the part they can.

- Your dopamine sources set your ceiling. Choose them like it matters. If what gives you dopamine doesn't build anything, you've built yourself a leak, not a habit.

- Most procrastination wears a costume: polished decks, fake plans, research tabs, premium UI kits. It's still fear. It's easier to make something look finished than to sit with not understanding it yet.

- Proof of work beats ideas, and it doesn't have to be pretty first. SpaceX's early rockets were ugly and they flew, the pretty part came later. An idea with no live URL is nothing, and a live URL with no users means ship harder. I've hired for senior roles. A GitHub link with no live demo gets skipped, nobody's cloning your repo to find out if it works.

- Genuine beats rehearsed. Mug up a few lines to impress someone and they'll clock the performance before they're impressed by anything in it.

- I'd rather break in public than polish in a private draft. I don't actually live up to this one. I've got documentary footage sitting in my gallery for months because some part of me is scared of being known for a certain kind of work if I post it unfinished.

- I'll keep shipping rough tools and weird bots until someone pays me to ship theirs faster. Two weekends after quitting my last job, two rough micro-SaaS builds, pitched to people I'd already worked with. They liked the core, asked for changes, started paying. No deck, no roadmap, just working software and a conversation.

- Code and media are the rare things that keep working after you stop touching them. One of those weekend builds is still bringing in money and I haven't touched the laptop in weeks. Same reason a creator's video from six months ago can suddenly take off and drag the whole channel with it.

- Read what the greats already worked out before assuming you're the first to hit the problem.

- You're always being shaped, by people and inputs, online and off. Pick both on purpose. You're the average of the five people closest to you, so treat that list like a decision, not an accident.

- Energy and time are the only currency you actually own.

- Urgency is fuel. Burnout is not the same thing, so don't mistake one for the other.

- "nothing helps; i must help myself, or i am finished" - Friedrich Nietzsche
