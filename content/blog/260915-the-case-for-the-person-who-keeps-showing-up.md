---
title: "The Case for the Person Who Keeps Showing Up"
date: "2026-09-15"
description: "A friend believes attraction is either there from the start or it never will be. Two people in my life only got close to me because they kept trying. I checked the research, and it backs each of us in different places."
tags: ["relationships", "people", "journal"]
---

A friend and I got into a long argument about attraction.

Their view goes like this. A lot of men approach relationships the way they approach work. Be useful, be supportive, be the nice friend, and eventually it turns into something. My friend thinks it doesn't work that way. Genuine attraction isn't negotiated. When someone is into you, you don't have to guess. If you catch yourself building reasons why they'll come around ("they just haven't seen enough of me yet"), you already have your answer.

So their rule is to filter first. Don't pour effort into someone who shows no interest at the start.

They had a second worry too. The slow path, where you talk to someone every day until you become a habit in their life, does sometimes work. But they've seen it go badly. People spend years on someone who was never interested. And the person who worked that hard to get in often holds on too tightly once they're there.

I agree with a lot of that. Nobody wants to spend years on someone who was never going to feel the same way.

But I pushed back on the rule itself. If everyone waits for the other person to show interest first, who starts anything?

I had two examples from my own life.

There was a guy who came up to talk to me every day. I wasn't talking to anyone much at the time. That's just my natural setting. I gave him almost nothing back. He kept coming anyway. Slowly we started actually talking, and now he's a really good friend. If he had gone by the signals I was giving, it never would have happened.

The second was a girl who kept inviting me to events. I kept not going. She kept inviting me. Eventually I went, and now we're connected. If she had read my silence as a no and stopped, there would be nothing there.

After the argument I went looking for what research actually says, mostly to find out which of us was wrong. The answer turned out to be both of us, in different places.

## Early signals are worse data than they feel like

The first thing I found is that people are bad at reading how much someone likes them. In a 2018 set of studies, [Boothby and colleagues](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30183512/) found that after a conversation, people consistently underestimate how much the other person liked them. They called it the liking gap. Among first-year students getting to know their dorm mates, it lasted for months.

I'm stretching the finding a little, since it's about reading others after a conversation. But if people routinely misjudge how much they're liked, a cold first read deserves less weight than it feels like it does. My own flat first signals were never a verdict on anyone. They were just me.

## Time together does real work, at least for friendship

The second thing is how much plain time matters. [Reis and colleagues](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0022885) paired up strangers and varied how much they interacted. The more they talked, the more they liked each other, mostly because they felt more understood and more comfortable.

And [Jeffrey Hall's work](https://doi.org/10.1177/0265407518761225) at the University of Kansas puts a rough number on it. By [his estimate](https://news.ku.edu/news/article/2018/03/06/study-reveals-number-hours-it-takes-make-friend), it takes about 50 hours together to go from acquaintance to casual friend, around 90 to become a friend, and more than 200 to become a close friend. Nobody gets to 50 hours on one conversation. Someone has to keep coming back.

So for friendship, the person who keeps showing up has decent evidence on their side.

## Where my friend is right

My friend's answer was that neither of my examples counts. Their rule was only ever about one specific situation, a man romantically pursuing a woman, and friendships don't follow the same logic.

The research gives them more ground here than I expected. The Reis studies deliberately used same-sex pairs because opposite-sex strangers get distracted by the dating question. So the cleanest evidence for "familiarity breeds liking" says little about romance.

There's also a real limit on exposure. In a 1977 experiment, [Walter Swap](https://doi.org/10.1177/014616727700300219) found that seeing someone more often made people like them more when that person was responsible for rewarding them. When the person was responsible for punishing them, the effect disappeared or turned slightly negative. Showing up more doesn't fix a bad impression. At best it does nothing, and it can make it a little worse. Even the Reis group, in a later [joint paper](https://doi.org/10.1177/1745691614561682) with researchers who had argued that familiarity breeds contempt, concluded that the effect of familiarity depends on the stage of the relationship.

Then there's my friend's second worry, the years spent on someone who was never interested. [Baumeister, Wotman and Stillwell](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.64.3.377) compared accounts from both sides of unrequited love. The would-be lovers tended to believe the attraction had been mutual, that they'd been led on, and that the rejection had never been clearly communicated. The people being pursued felt guilty about hurting someone, and many described the persistent efforts as intrusive and annoying. Both sides were miserable, and neither understood the other.

That's the failure my friend is describing, almost exactly.

I did find one thing that pushes back on the rule for romance. In a [study of 167 couples](https://doi.org/10.1177/0956797615579273), partners who got together soon after meeting were more closely matched on physical attractiveness. Couples who had known each other longer, or had been friends first, were less matched. That doesn't prove attraction grows out of nothing. It does suggest the early read isn't fixed.

## Two kinds of persistence

So the real line isn't friendship versus romance. It's between two kinds of persistence.

One ignores a clear no and tries to wear someone down. The unrequited love research shows how that goes for both people. The other just keeps a door open, with low pressure and good company, the kind of repeated contact that actually builds liking.

The guy who kept talking to me never pressured me into anything. He was friendly and he was around, and he left the rest up to me. Based on everything I read, that's close to the best case for how familiarity works.

Low interest at the start isn't always a no. Sometimes it's just someone who's slow to open up. I'm that person. Under my friend's rule, most people would have walked away from me, and a couple of the people I value most would never have become part of my life.

Maybe the rule isn't "only invest in people who already like you." Maybe it's "don't keep investing in someone who has told you no, and make sure you'd actually hear it if they did." Those sound close. They lead to very different lives.
