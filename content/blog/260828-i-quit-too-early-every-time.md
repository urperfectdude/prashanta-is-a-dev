date: "2026-08-28"

If you ask me for my biggest mistake, I don't have to think about it. It's not a decision. It's a pattern: the moment a problem gets slow, I go find an easier one.

I've changed roles more times than I want to list here. Design, video editing, an agency, an engineering job I left in a month, hardware products, a social app I worked on for six months before admitting it wasn't going anywhere. Different situations on paper, same trigger underneath. Progress slows down, and I start looking for the exit instead of pushing through the slow part.

I used to call this flexibility. Trying things, staying open, not getting stuck. Some of that is real. But flexibility that only ever points away from difficulty isn't openness. It's avoidance wearing a nicer name.

Here's what I think actually happens when you stay on one hard problem past the point where it stopped being fun: you build a kind of thinking that switching never asks of you. The people who are genuinely excellent at something have usually spent long, boring stretches on a single problem that refused to resolve quickly. That's not a coincidence. Staying is the mechanism, not a side effect of it.

I'm not saying I've fixed this. I notice it happening in real time now, which is not the same thing as stopping it. I still feel the same pull the moment something gets hard and slow, the urge to go be productive somewhere easier instead. Naming a pattern doesn't remove it. It just means I catch myself a little earlier each time.

I don't know if there's a clean way out of this one. Maybe the only real test is the next hard problem, and whether I'm still there once it stops being interesting.
