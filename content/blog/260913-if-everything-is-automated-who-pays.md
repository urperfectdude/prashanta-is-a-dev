The fear about AI is usually framed as a question about workers. What happens to people when machines do their jobs?

There's a quieter question underneath it that I think matters more. If machines do all the jobs, who buys anything?

## The paradox

A friend put it simply on a call. Companies automate to cut costs and sell more. But the people they stop paying are also their customers. Automate far enough and you get factories that can make everything, and a population with no wages to buy it.

There's an old story where Henry Ford's grandson shows a union leader a new automated plant and asks how he'll get robots to pay union dues. The union leader asks back how Ford will get robots to buy cars. The story is probably made up. It keeps getting retold because the question in it has never had a clean answer.

A single company can automate without worrying about this, because its workers are a tiny share of its customers. The problem only shows up when everyone does it at once. Each decision makes sense alone. Together they remove the demand they all depend on.

## We already have enough

The second idea from that conversation stuck with me longer.

In 1930, the economist John Maynard Keynes predicted that his grandchildren's generation would work around fifteen hours a week, because productivity would have solved the basic problem of having enough.

He was roughly right about the productivity. He was wrong about the hours.

For a lot of people in richer countries, the basics are covered in a way that would have looked like fantasy a hundred years ago. More innovation keeps coming, but a lot of it doesn't make daily life much better. A faster phone doesn't feed anyone more than the last phone did.

My friend's view was that we've hit a kind of saturation point for basic living, and people still work full time because the system needs them to. Work is now how money gets distributed, how people get a sense of place and how the next generation learns to keep the whole thing running.

I think there's something to that, though I'm not sure how far it goes. Plenty of the world is nowhere near saturation, including large parts of India. But it does explain why "machines can do the work" doesn't lead straight to "people stop working."

## Why nobody slows down

If the risks are obvious, why not slow down?

Because at every level, stopping means losing.

Companies building AI say openly that they're not sure where it's going. Some of their leaders have asked for regulation. But a lab that pauses alone just hands its lead to one that doesn't. So they ask governments to make everyone stop together, knowing that's unlikely.

Governments face the same trap one level up. A US law that slows American AI mostly helps China, and the reverse is also true. Neither side wants to be the one that fell behind by choice.

We've seen this shape before. In the Cold War, the US and the Soviet Union raced to space and to the Moon. That race was about showing the world whose system worked. AI has become the new version: a way for a country to prove, and project, power.

No single actor can stop a race like that. And the players who most understand the risk are the ones with the strongest reason to keep running.

## What I actually think

I don't know how this plays out. I'm suspicious of anyone who says they do.

Maybe new kinds of work appear, the way they did after earlier waves of automation. Maybe governments redistribute some of the gains, through something like a basic income. Maybe the transition is messy and unequal for a long time before it settles.

What I'm fairly sure of is this. The paradox doesn't get solved by the companies automating, because solving it isn't their job. It gets solved, or not, by whoever ends up deciding how the gains are shared.

That's a political question. And almost all the attention right now is on the technical side.
