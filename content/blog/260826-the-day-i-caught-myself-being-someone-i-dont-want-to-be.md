---
title: "The Day I Caught Myself Being Someone I Don't Want to Be"
date: "2026-08-26"
description: "A pickleball court, an empty second court, and a caretaker's question that made me see how easily I can rationalize doing the exact opposite of what I claim to believe."
tags: ["journal", "mindset", "life"]
---

Today I did something that has been bothering me more than I expected. It was just a pickleball court. Six hundred rupees. An empty court. A few extra people. Nothing that sounds serious when you describe it out loud. But that's exactly why it bothered me.

The last time we booked the court, there was no caretaker around. Other people were playing past their booked time, and eventually we did too. We had paid for an hour and stayed for more than an hour and a half.

That's when something shifted in my head. I started thinking, *nobody is coming anyway, why not?* One of the guys playing that day said something like, "Just keep playing until someone tells you to stop. Nobody is going to come." That line planted a seed. We started talking about booking less time next time and just continuing until somebody stopped us.

Today, when I went to book, I found out the minimum was one hour anyway, so I paid for one court. We were supposed to be six people. Two more showed up. The caretaker saw eight of us and said if all eight were playing, we'd need to pay for both courts. I told him we'd stick to one court and rotate. That was genuinely the plan.

Then, while we were playing, another group walked onto the second court and started using it. I didn't stop them. Some part of me was completely fine with it, because the court was sitting there empty anyway.

The caretaker came back and called me over, since I was the one who'd made the booking. He asked why we were playing on both courts. I told him, smiling, that the court was empty so we figured we'd use it.

He got angry. He compared it to walking into a hotel and asking for a free room because it happened to be empty. The comparison wasn't really the point he was making, and I knew it. The point was that the court wasn't ours just because nobody else was in it.

I didn't have much to say. He kept asking if I had any shame. That landed harder than I expected, not because someone was shouting at me, but because somewhere underneath it, I knew he was right.

I've spent a fair amount of time telling people they should be genuine, that they shouldn't take advantage of trust, that actions matter even when nobody's watching, that you should keep your side of the ledger clean. Today, when nobody was watching, I did the opposite of every bit of that.

For a second I actually asked myself: what kind of person am I?

It's easy to hold principles when following them costs you nothing. It's easy to be honest when there's a camera, a rule, a caretaker, or some punishment sitting there waiting. The real test shows up when something is just sitting available, nobody seems to care, and you stand to gain from taking it anyway. Today I failed that test.

I don't think the honest conclusion here is "I'm a bad person." That's too easy, and it lets me off the hook by turning this into an identity instead of a choice. The more uncomfortable conclusion is that I'm capable of rationalizing things I already know are wrong.

Wrongdoing rarely shows up announcing itself. It shows up dressed as logic: nobody's using it, everyone else is doing it, there's no caretaker anyway, it's only for a few minutes, why waste something that's already sitting empty. String enough of those together and something that would look obviously wrong from the outside starts to feel completely reasonable from inside your own head.

That's the part worth paying attention to.

Today wasn't some huge moral failure. It was a small mirror, and I didn't love what I saw in it. I don't want to be someone who's only honest when there's someone around to enforce it. I want to be the same person when there's nobody there to catch me.

So I'm not filing this away as "I'm a thief" or "I'm a hypocrite." I'm filing it as a reminder: my character isn't what I say I believe, it's what I actually do when I could get away with doing otherwise. Today gave me a small, clear lesson in that.

---

After sitting with it, I messaged the caretaker directly:

> I wanted to genuinely apologize for what happened today. After thinking about it properly, I realized I was wrong.
>
> I had already noticed earlier that there was no caretaker around and started thinking we could get away with using the court beyond what we'd booked. Today, when the second court was empty, I carried that same mindset forward and thought it was fine to use it since nobody else was on it.
>
> Even though we were stopped and I eventually asked everyone to get off the second court, the intention itself was wrong. I was trying to take advantage of the situation instead of respecting the booking rules, and I shouldn't have done that.
>
> I also understand that you're responsible for managing the courts, and that letting one person use an empty court for free isn't fair to the business or to the other customers who do pay for it.
>
> I'm sorry for arguing instead of just accepting that I was wrong when you confronted me. I genuinely felt bad about it afterward, and I wanted to acknowledge that instead of letting it go.
>
> I'll make sure it doesn't happen again. Sorry for the trouble today.
