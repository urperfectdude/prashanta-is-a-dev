Block cut close to half its workforce, about 4,000 people, and called it an AI reorg. A Czech insurance company called Direct just fired a third of its 1,200 employees. Its CEO didn't dress it up: "The time of playing around with AI is over."

Everyone reads this as a jobs story, but it's also a products story. Once software gets mostly written by AI, the thing itself changes shape, not just the people who build it. Four ways.

## A low barrier makes a low-respect field

Nobody argues with you about how you're proving a theorem. Everybody has an opinion on your landing page colors. The difference isn't the subject, it's who can evaluate the work. Math stays sealed off because outsiders can't read the notation. Marketing stays exposed because anyone can judge it.

![Closed fields like mathematics, physics, and 2015-era software engineering versus open fields like marketing, design, and content creation, with software crossing from closed to open in the AI era](/projects/software-to-marketing.png)

Software used to sit closer to math, a closed field judged by other engineers. Now that anyone can vibecode a working app in an afternoon, it's sliding toward marketing's side of the line. Cheap to enter and easy to judge from the outside means lower respect, and pay compresses right behind it. What doesn't compress is proven, visible skill: a specialization, a track record, something a client can point to. The middle drains out. The premium on the people still worth hiring goes up.

## Infinite options kill commitment

Dating apps didn't just add options, they removed the reason to stop looking. When there are thousands of possible matches, "good enough" stops feeling like a reason to commit.

Software is heading the same way. Three tools meant you picked one and lived with it. Three hundred tools, with a fourth you could build yourself before lunch, means commitment stops making sense. Treat software like water: abundant, and beneath anyone's loyalty.

Barry Schwartz called this the paradox of choice: more options, less satisfaction. Flood a market with alternatives and buyers stop settling. They go looking for something built just for them, and may never find it.

## The middle class of software disappears

This is the App Store playbook, one level up. Software used to reward being the only one who could do a specific thing well. Once anyone can clone that thing over a weekend, the mid-sized vendor charging fifty dollars a month has no moat: too expensive to beat a free AI-built alternative, too small to beat a platform's network effects.

Underneath that, a long tail explodes: a freelancer builds their own invoicing tool, a teacher builds their own grading tool. Not businesses, personal utilities, millions of them. Above it, a handful of aggregators absorb everything else, because trust and distribution are the only things left that don't commoditize. Vibecode your own note-taking app tonight, and it still needs someone's cloud and someone's ecosystem to be found in.

## Sell the outcome, not the tool

For every dollar a company spends on software, it spends roughly six on the people who operate it. SaaS spent two decades chasing that first dollar. AI opens up the second one, at software margins.

A company pays ten thousand dollars a year for accounting software and a hundred and twenty thousand for an accountant. The next big company in that space won't sell better software. It will just close the books. The same logic frees the accountant: once AI absorbs the junior work, one person can sell judgment to two hundred clients instead of sitting at one desk, because judgment was never the part that needed a desk.

## Where this leaves you

If you write software for a living, welcome to what non-technical people have dealt with for years: confident amateurs second-guessing your decisions. Either be undeniably the best at the craft, or pair it with something the craft alone can't give you: design sense, domain depth. Competent and nothing else is now the worst seat in the room.

If you don't write software, you can build things now, but code was never the hard part. Taste and distribution were, and AI hasn't touched either. If you're building a company, stop selling the tool and sell the outcome it used to make possible. The tool is a commodity now. The judgment behind it isn't.

The barrier to entry for software just fell, and it isn't going back up. The real question isn't whether this changes the field. It's whether you're the one people still pay for judgment, or just another person who can now type code into existence like everyone else.
