We assume money, once made, stays made. A family gets rich and their grandchildren are set.

Most of the evidence points the other way.

A friend brought this up while we were talking about why some families stay wealthy for centuries and most don't. There's an old saying for it in almost every culture. In English it's "shirtsleeves to shirtsleeves in three generations." In Chinese, roughly, "wealth doesn't last past three generations."

## Build, keep, spend

The pattern goes like this.

The first generation builds. They usually start with little, take risks, work absurd hours and know exactly what every rupee cost them.

The second generation grew up watching that. They may not have built it, but they saw how hard it was. They tend to protect it.

The third generation grew up inside the wealth. The money was always there. The discipline that created it is a family story, not something they lived through. That's usually where it starts to go.

It isn't a law. There are families that break the pattern for a long time, and India has a few business houses that are well past their third generation and still huge. But they are the exceptions people remember, not the rule.

## Division does the rest

Even without anyone wasting a rupee, inheritance splits the money.

Say a founder has two children. Each gets half. If each of them has two children, those four get a quarter each. By the fourth generation, one fortune has become eight or sixteen smaller ones, held by cousins who may barely know each other and don't share goals.

John D. Rockefeller was the richest man in America in his time. His descendants today number in the hundreds. As a family, they are still wealthy. But no single Rockefeller today holds anything close to what he did. The fortune didn't vanish. It thinned out until it stopped being one thing.

## Windfalls go faster

If inherited wealth drains over decades, sudden wealth can go in a few years.

In 2009, Sports Illustrated reported that a large share of NFL players were bankrupt or under serious financial stress within two years of retiring, and many NBA players within five. Those numbers get quoted everywhere, and they are probably too high. A later academic study of NFL players found bankruptcy rates closer to one in six within twelve years of retirement. That's lower, but for people who earned millions, it's still striking.

Lottery research points the same way. A study of Florida lottery winners found that bigger wins mostly delayed bankruptcy for people who were already struggling, rather than preventing it.

India has its own well-known case. The first contestant to win the ₹5 crore prize on Kaun Banega Crorepati later wrote publicly about how he went broke in the years after, through failed business attempts, people taking advantage of him and giving a lot of it away.

The pattern is similar in all three. Getting money and knowing how to keep it are separate skills, and the windfall only gives you the first.

## The equal money thought experiment

My friend pushed this one step further.

Imagine you took all the wealth in the world and gave everyone the same amount. How long before inequality came back?

Probably not very long. Some people would spend it all. Some would save. Some would start something, hire the spenders and grow what they had. Within a few years, you'd see a gap again, and within a generation it might look a lot like today.

This is a thought experiment, not data, and it hides a lot. Real inequality also comes from inherited advantages, connections, laws and plain luck, and redistribution does change outcomes in the real world. But the experiment makes one point hard to dodge: money moves toward people and systems that hold on to it, and away from those that don't.

## What this means for normal people

I don't come from wealth, so the three generations problem isn't mine directly. But the lesson under it applies at any income.

Money is not a stock you have. It's a flow you manage. A salary, a bonus, a big contract, an inheritance: none of it stays unless there is a habit keeping it there.

The first generation's real asset was never the money. It was knowing what the money cost. If that knowledge doesn't get passed down, the money won't stay either.
