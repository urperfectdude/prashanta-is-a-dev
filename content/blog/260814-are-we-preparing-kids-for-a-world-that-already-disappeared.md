By sending a kid to school, are we preparing them for the world they'll graduate into, or for the one that already stopped existing?

I dropped out of IIT Madras, so take this from someone with a complicated relationship with formal education. But the question got sharper for me over the past year, not because of the dropout, because of a keyboard shortcut.

At twelve or thirteen, I learned CorelDRAW and Photoshop the way other kids learn to ride a bike: obsessively, alone, figuring out layers by trial and error. I thought getting fast at Photoshop was the skill. Years in, I understood it wasn't. The skill was knowing what looks good and why, understanding what a person needs before they can tell you, turning a fuzzy idea into something someone else can see. Photoshop was the pencil. I'd mistaken it for the hand.

That mistake is now the default setting of most classrooms. School still optimizes for the pencil: memorize the material, produce the right answer, repeat it back under exam conditions. That approach made sense when the pencil was scarce, when getting access to information at all was the hard part. It stopped making sense the day information became free and instant, and it's actively wrong now that AI can produce the pencil's entire output on command.

Watch what's happening to the tools I spent years learning. Coding, writing, video editing, research, first-pass design: a lot of what used to take years to get good at, AI now does in minutes. A small team with the right tools can put out work that used to need a much bigger one. The work still needs a human to decide what's worth doing, judge what's actually good, and make the final call. But producing the raw material got cheap, fast, everywhere.

That's the part school hasn't caught up to. If a tool can write the code, draft the design, edit the video, and summarize the research, then knowing the tool is only half the problem, arguably the smaller half. The real question was never "can you use Photoshop." It was always "do you know what's worth making." AI just made that gap impossible to hide behind competence with the tool.

I don't have a clean fix. But I know what I wish I'd spent more of my own school years on: asking a better question instead of memorizing the answer to an old one, building something from nothing, explaining an idea to someone who doesn't already agree with you, sitting with a problem nobody's going to hand you the answer to, starting over at zero without treating it as a threat. None of that expires. The tool a twelve-year-old learns this year probably won't exist by the time they're twenty-five. I'm the proof: I went from CorelDRAW to a world where AI can do things that would have sounded like science fiction when I was that age.

So maybe the fix isn't a new subject. It's a different question underneath the old one. Less "did you get the right answer," more "did you ask a question worth asking." Less remembering, more making: small projects, real writing, a website that breaks and gets fixed, a tiny business that fails in an instructive way. Kids doing something because they're curious about it, not because there's a test on Friday.

We don't know what a twenty-five-year-old's job will look like when today's twelve-year-olds get there. Neither do the people writing the curriculum. What we can still teach is how to learn anything from a standing start, how to think in public, how to work with other people, how to tell a real idea from a borrowed one. That doesn't go out of date. Everything else might.

I'm not worried that kids aren't learning. I'm worried we're spending their most curious years training them for a world that already left.
