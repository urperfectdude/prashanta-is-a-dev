title: "Why the Paid Mentorship Calls Didn't Help"
date: "2026-09-07"
description: "I booked three or four paid calls on a mentorship platform at around Rs. 500 each and came away with nothing I could use. The mentors weren't the problem. How I picked them was."
tags: ["career", "mindset", "people"]
---

When I started looking at product roles, a friend suggested I talk to people who had already done it. So I went to a mentorship platform and booked some calls.

Three, maybe four sessions. I picked people who were rated highly and fit my budget, around Rs. 500 a call.

I didn't get anything out of them. Not bad advice exactly. Just nothing specific to me, nothing I couldn't have found on my own. By the time I was thinking about a fifth one, I didn't feel like spending the money again.

When I told my friend, their first question wasn't about the mentors. It was: where did you find them?

I searched on the platform and booked from the top of the list. It was the easiest way, so that's what I did.

"So there's a problem, right?" they said. The platform shows you the people who do well on the platform. People who are pleasant to talk to, who reply fast, who collect good reviews. Those are selling skills. They don't tell you whether the person has done the thing you're trying to do.

I'm not blaming the people I spoke to. They showed up and they were polite, and some of them are probably good at their actual jobs. But the ranking isn't built to find that out, and I let the ranking do my thinking for me.

The people I actually wanted to talk to were on LinkedIn, people who had made the move I'm trying to make. They charged Rs. 2,000 to 3,000 a session. At the time that felt like too much, so I skipped them and went cheap. Add up what I spent on the cheap calls and it's most of one session with someone who has actually made the switch.

Then my friend said something I didn't expect. They don't charge anyone. People message them regularly asking how they got into their role, and they just get on a call and talk. They said they could probably charge Rs. 1,000 a call tomorrow and people would pay. They still don't want to. They were clear that people who charge aren't doing anything wrong. It's just not something they want to do.

That changed how I think about finding advice. The people who are easiest to find are the ones putting effort into being found. The ones who are good at the work are either charging a fair price for their time or not selling it at all, and the second kind only shows up through people you already know.

Next time, before paying anyone, I'll ask the people around me one question first: who would you talk to?
