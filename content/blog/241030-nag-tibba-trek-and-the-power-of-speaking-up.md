title: "Nag Tibba Trek and the Power of Speaking Up"
date: "2024-10-30"
description: "A short travel moment during the Nag Tibba trek that turned into a deeper lesson on group silence, discomfort, and the courage to ask for what is needed."

Nag Tibba was one of those trips that keeps replaying in memory even after months. I started the trek with a group and a guide, but the pace was slow for me, so I kept moving steadily and ended up reaching the summit first. That feeling of standing there before the crowd arrived, breathing cold mountain air with full silence around me, is still one of my favorite trek moments.

What made this trek even more interesting was the local folklore around it. I heard a story linked to Dand Shrikhanda temple, where people say Mata Rani threw a trishul from Shrikhanda. The place where the trishul landed became Dand Shrikhanda, and where the nag fell became Nag Tibba. I do not know the historical accuracy, but hearing these stories while walking those trails gives the landscape a very different depth.

Near the route, I also heard that Goat Village used to host a local Gost Mela years ago, but over time the place shifted more toward private ownership and commercialization. Somewhere around one kilometer above Pantwari, I met a girl named Amrita while trekking upward, and that conversation triggered another thought in my head. Why do so many people love mountain views enough to climb, but once they reach, many treat the whole trek like a picnic or a photoshoot? The view is beautiful, but the trek also has a principle, patience, effort, silence, and self-observation. I came back thinking the mountain gives much more than photos if we let it.

This was also my first proper snow experience. I had seen snow before only in photos and videos, but touching it, falling in it, and laughing like a kid in it is something else. We even did improvised skiing using tree bark, which was chaotic and hilarious at the same time. We had proper snow fights too, with everyone pretending to be serious trekkers one minute and absolute children the next.

The Himalayan views from there were unreal. On a clear stretch we could spot Bandarpoonch, Swargarohini, Kala Nag, Srikantha, parts of the Gangotri group, Kedarnath, and Chaukhamba in the distance. Watching those peaks slowly light up was surreal, and the sunrise that morning is still the best sunrise I have seen till date.

While descending, I met a small kid on the way who kept calling me to drink lemonade. I was not very interested at first, but I still stopped because she had asked me the previous day too while I was climbing. I sat at their small stall and saw that they were four sisters. The elder one was preparing lemonade, and the smallest one was calling trekkers from the trail. During our conversation I noticed that the little sister had only one eye working, and their family could not afford treatment for the other eye. She was very cute, very smart, and surprisingly confident while speaking to strangers. The lemonade was genuinely so good that I ordered it twice. While leaving, I kept thinking if I could do something for her. Then another thought came that there are thousands of families facing similar struggles, and I cannot help everyone right now. I left with one clear promise to myself that once I have enough money and capacity, I will definitely try to help people like her in a meaningful way.

One more moment from the same trip gave me a completely different lesson. While we were traveling in a Bolero on steep roads, the motion was intense. I was feeling motion sickness, and I could see at least three or four other people struggling too. Everyone looked uncomfortable, but no one was saying anything.

After a while, I asked the driver to stop for a few minutes because I was not feeling well. The moment the vehicle stopped, three people immediately started vomiting. That scene stayed with me, not because anyone was weak, but because all of us were quietly suffering while waiting for someone else to speak first.

It made me think how often this happens in life. In groups, people think they should not disturb others, or maybe they are the only one feeling something, or speaking up will make them look difficult. So everyone tolerates discomfort longer than necessary.

That day I did not do anything extraordinary. I just asked the driver to stop. But that one sentence helped multiple people. Since then, I remind myself that asking clearly is better than silently enduring, and communicating early is better than escalating pain. Nag Tibba gave me snow, views, and adventure, but the biggest thing I brought back was this: most people are waiting for someone to speak first.
