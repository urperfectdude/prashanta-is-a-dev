---
title: "Why I Ended a Long Collaboration"
date: "2025-02-08"
description: "A personal reflection on gratitude, growth, money commitments, and the difficult decision to end a long mentor collaboration without resentment."
tags: ["career", "journal", "mindset", "product"]
---

This is one of the hardest career decisions I have made so far.

I started working with a mentor when I was around 17. At that stage, he gave me a real opportunity when I had very little proof of work. I joined as a part-time video editor with a light workload and modest pay, and over time the trust grew. I got involved in far more than editing, including marketing experiments, product execution, design, and early startup building.

As the years passed, the role kept expanding. At one point I had strong external offers in hand, including a stable technical track and better compensation options, but I stayed because I believed in the long-term journey we were building together. That decision taught me commitment, but it also taught me how difficult it is to balance loyalty with personal direction.

We later attempted startup progress for many months, and when execution slowed down, I moved to another company role to improve my skills and career trajectory. Even then, I continued part-time support to avoid leaving things abruptly. Around this period, I also took financial help for a MacBook loan and continued paying the EMI consistently, which added another layer of responsibility to the relationship.

The turning point came when expectations, communication style, and career priorities started diverging. A few conversations became emotionally charged, and trust was clearly getting weaker. I tried to stay professional by offering task completion and knowledge transfer so ongoing work would not suffer, but internally I knew I could not continue from a place of guilt and confusion.

Later, when I explored rejoining options, compensation and role clarity became another friction point. I asked for revised terms based on market direction and my current trajectory, and the conversation ended with visible tension. At that moment I realized the relationship had shifted from constructive mentorship to recurring stress.

The hardest part was not saying no. The hardest part was saying no without disrespect.

I did not want to carry bitterness because this person had genuinely helped me at a stage when very few people did. At the same time, gratitude cannot mean permanent career compromise. Respect should be mutual, and growth decisions should not require emotional guilt.

So I chose to step away.

Not because everything was bad, but because the next chapter required a different environment, clearer ownership, and healthier communication.

What I learned from this:

Early supporters deserve gratitude.
Long collaboration needs evolving boundaries.
Financial dependency should always be documented clearly.
Loyalty matters, but self-direction matters too.

I still wish him well. I carry learning, not resentment.

Sometimes the most honest way to protect a relationship is to end the working arrangement before both sides lose respect.
